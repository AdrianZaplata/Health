## Question

How well does the evidence support the working hypothesis (habitual leg crossing + leaning → lateral pelvic tilt / pelvic torsion → chronic low back pain), and what do current clinical guidelines actually recommend for chronic non-specific low back pain?

Specifically:

1. What does peer-reviewed evidence say about the link between postural asymmetries (pelvic tilt, leg crossing, sitting posture) and low back pain? Is the causal chain solid, contested, or refuted?
2. What is the clinical standing of "lower crossed syndrome" and "gluteal amnesia" — established diagnoses or contested constructs?
3. What do the major current guidelines (WHO 2023 low back pain guideline, NICE NG59, ACP, Lancet low back pain series) recommend for chronic non-specific low back pain — especially the role of exercise, and which kinds?
4. What red flags should send someone with recurrent immobilizing episodes to a doctor rather than a gym?

Findings go to `wayfinder/research/002-posture-evidence.md`, every claim cited to its primary source.

## Resolution

Findings in [`wayfinder/research/002-posture-evidence.md`](../research/002-posture-evidence.md), fully cited. Gist — and it cuts against the working hypothesis:

- **The causal chain is unsupported.** Umbrella and meta-analytic evidence (Swain 2020; Sannasi 2024; Nourbakhsh & Arab 2002) finds no consistent association between lateral pelvic tilt, leg-length/hip-flexor asymmetry, or habitual leg crossing and chronic low back pain; leg crossing produces only transient positional effects.
- **"Lower crossed syndrome" and "gluteal amnesia" are unvalidated clinical narratives**, not diagnoses. Where glute inhibition is real, the documented mechanism runs *pain → inhibition* (arthrogenic inhibition), not *sitting → amnesia*.
- **Guidelines converge** (WHO 2023, NICE NG59, ACP 2017, Lancet 2018 series): stay active; exercise is first-line for chronic non-specific LBP with no single modality clearly superior — resistance training qualifies; no routine imaging, braces, traction, or bed rest.
- **Red flags** requiring a doctor first: bladder/bowel/saddle symptoms (emergency), progressive leg weakness, trauma, steroid use, cancer history, unexplained weight loss, fever, unrelenting night pain, inflammatory pattern. The described presentation matches none, but one professional assessment remains advised given the recurrent immobilizing episodes.
- **The one supported piece of the narrative**: low trunk-muscle endurance/deconditioning is the strongest *trainable* correlate of LBP — so the gym plan is evidence-aligned even though the self-diagnosed mechanism is not.

Consequence for the map: the destination stands (train, eat, sleep, assess), but downstream tickets should build on "get strong and stay active" rather than "correct the tilt/torsion". [Decide the rehab and mobility protocol](006-decide-rehab-protocol.md) inherits the job of reconciling this with whatever the professional assessment finds.
