## Question

What do high-trust sources say about how a beginner with a history of recurrent low back pain should structure their first months of gym training?

Specifically:

1. Is progressive resistance training safe and effective for people with chronic/recurrent low back pain, per the evidence?
2. What established frameworks exist for building spine tolerance first (e.g. McGill's "Big 3", hip-hinge patterning progressions), and what does the evidence say about them?
3. Which common gym movements are typically modified, delayed, or substituted early on for this population, and what are the standard progressions back to full versions?
4. What does a sensible beginner template look like (frequency, full-body vs split, volume, progression scheme) per strength-training literature — independent of the back issue?
5. How is glute/hip strengthening typically programmed for people whose pain narrative involves hip-flexor tightness and glute inhibition?

Findings go to `wayfinder/research/003-training-with-lbp.md`, every claim cited to its primary source.

## Resolution

Findings in [`wayfinder/research/003-training-with-lbp.md`](../research/003-training-with-lbp.md), fully cited. Gist:

- **Progressive resistance training is safe and effective** for chronic/recurrent non-specific LBP (Cochrane 2021, moderate certainty; Owen 2020 network meta-analysis ranks resistance among the top exercise modes; 2025 meta-analyses confirm loaded training is safe and feasible).
- **McGill's staged framework** (movement pattern → endurance "Big 3" → strength → power) is biomechanically grounded but thin on RCT support. Swedish RCTs show deadlift-based rehab works, with a concrete readiness gate: Biering–Sørensen extensor endurance > 60 s and pain < 60/100 predict who benefits from loading.
- **Early modifications**: delay floor barbell deadlifts and heavy back squats via trap-bar/elevated/goblet progressions (trap bar has lower lumbar moments — Swinton 2011); avoid *loaded end-range* flexion early — though flexion-during-lifting is not an established LBP risk factor (Saraceni 2020).
- **Beginner template** (ACSM 2009 + Schoenfeld meta-analyses): full-body 2–3×/week, 2–3 sets of 8–12 reps of compounds, each muscle twice a week, double progression (+2–10% load) — which matches the ≥12-week, ~3×/week dose that predicts LBP pain improvement.
- **Hip/glute strengthening measurably improves LBP** pain and disability (2023 systematic reviews), programmed as normal progressive loading (hip thrust/RDL plus abduction accessories). The "tight hip flexors → anterior tilt → dead glutes" story is unsupported — keep the exercises, drop the narrative.
