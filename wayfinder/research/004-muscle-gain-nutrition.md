# 004 — Nutrition targets for muscle gain in an untrained adult (with chronic sleep deprivation as a modifier)

Researched: 2026-08-14. Sources are meta-analyses, position stands, and mainstream guidelines; each claim is cited inline.

## Bottom line

- **Protein: ~1.6 g/kg body weight/day** (plateau in the best meta-analysis); 1.6–2.2 g/kg is a safe, defensible range while gaining. Distribution is a second-order refinement: aim for ~0.4 g/kg (roughly 25–40 g) per meal across 3–5 meals; total daily intake matters far more than timing.
- **Calorie surplus: small — roughly +200–400 kcal/day (~5–15% over maintenance)**, targeting **~0.25–0.5% body-weight gain per week**. As a true beginner you can expect meaningful muscle even at the low end; bigger surpluses mostly add fat, not extra muscle.
- **Estimate maintenance** with Mifflin-St Jeor × activity factor as a starting guess, then **calibrate against the scale**: weigh daily, average weekly, and adjust intake ±100–200 kcal every 2–3 weeks based on the measured trend (≈7,700 kcal ≈ 1 kg of body weight).
- **Health base first**: a whole-food dietary pattern (vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, dairy/fish), fiber at ~14 g per 1,000 kcal (≈ 28–34 g/day), food before supplements — per the Dietary Guidelines for Americans and the AND/DC/ACSM position.
- **Only two supplements are worth considering**: creatine monohydrate 3–5 g/day (strongest evidence of any supplement for lean mass and strength, well-established safety) and vitamin D **only if blood level is low** (correct deficiency; supplementing a replete person does not add strength).
- **Sleep is a first-class training variable**: sleep restriction measurably lowers muscle protein synthesis (~18–19% in controlled studies), shifts weight change toward fat and away from lean mass, raises ghrelin/lowers leptin (more hunger, worse food choices), and increases pain sensitivity. Target ≥7 h/night; treat improving sleep as part of the program, not an optional extra.
- Practical hedge for poor-sleep periods: keep protein at the top of the range, keep the surplus at the small end (appetite dysregulation + fat-partitioning risk), and expect elevated soreness/pain to be partly a sleep signal rather than purely a training signal.

## 1. Daily protein and per-meal distribution

**Daily amount.** The Morton et al. 2018 meta-analysis (49 RCTs, n=1,863) found protein supplementation augments resistance-training gains in fat-free mass and strength, with a dose–response plateau at **~1.62 g/kg/day** (95% CI upper bound ~2.2 g/kg/day); beyond that, no further lean-mass benefit. Total daily intake mattered more than source or timing.
- Morton RW et al., 2018. "A systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression of the effect of protein supplementation on resistance training-induced gains in muscle mass and strength in healthy adults." *Br J Sports Med* 52(6):376–384. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/

The ISSN position stand recommends **1.4–2.0 g/kg/day** for building and maintaining muscle in exercising people — consistent with Morton. The bodybuilding-specific review by Iraki et al. lands on **1.6–2.2 g/kg/day** for a gaining phase.
- Jäger R et al., 2017. "International Society of Sports Nutrition Position Stand: protein and exercise." *JISSN* 14:20. https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8
- Iraki J, Fitschen P, Espinar S, Helms E, 2019. "Nutrition Recommendations for Bodybuilders in the Off-Season: A Narrative Review." *Sports (Basel)* 7(7):154. https://doi.org/10.3390/sports7070154

For a 75 kg beginner: **~120 g protein/day** (range 120–165 g).

**Per-meal distribution.** ISSN: ~0.25 g/kg high-quality protein per meal (absolute 20–40 g), ideally every 3–4 h, with each dose supplying ~700–3,000 mg leucine. Schoenfeld & Aragon argue the practical per-meal ceiling is higher — **~0.4 g/kg/meal across ≥4 meals** — and Iraki et al. recommend 0.40–0.55 g/kg/meal over 3–6 meals. Consensus reading: distribution is a refinement worth maybe a few percent; hitting the daily total is the dominant factor (Morton found timing did not moderate outcomes).
- Schoenfeld BJ, Aragon AA, 2018. "How much protein can the body use in a single meal for muscle-building?" *JISSN* 15:10. https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-018-0215-z

**Practical rule:** 3–5 meals/day each containing ~25–40 g protein, one of them within a couple hours after training (convenience, not magic).

## 2. Calorie surplus for a beginner

**Is a surplus needed, and how big?** A surplus accelerates lean gain, but the relationship saturates quickly. Slater et al.'s review concludes an energy surplus supports maximal hypertrophy but that large surpluses disproportionately add fat (classic overfeeding data: ~2 kg fat per 1 kg lean when surplus is aggressive and untargeted). The best direct trial (trained lifters, 8 weeks) compared maintenance vs ~5% vs ~15% surplus: faster weight gain **increased skinfolds (fat) without additional strength or muscle-thickness gains**.
- Slater GJ et al., 2019. "Is an Energy Surplus Required to Maximize Skeletal Muscle Hypertrophy Associated With Resistance Training?" *Front Nutr* 6:131. https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnut.2019.00131/full
- Helms ER et al., 2023. "Effect of Small and Large Energy Surpluses on Strength, Muscle, and Skinfold Thickness in Resistance-Trained Individuals." *Sports Med Open* 9:102. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-023-00651-y

**Recommended target.** Iraki et al. (novice/intermediate): eat **~10–20% above maintenance**, aiming for **~0.25–0.5% of body weight gained per week** (for 75 kg: ~0.2–0.4 kg/week; in practice roughly +200–400 kcal/day). Beginners can use the upper half of that gain-rate range because untrained lifters partition nutrients toward muscle better; lean-toward-lower if fat gain is a concern. Conversely, avoid dieting hard while trying to build: a meta-analysis shows energy deficit blunts resistance-training lean-mass gains (deficits ≳500 kcal/day largely prevent them).
- Iraki et al. 2019 (above).
- Murphy C, Koehler K, 2022. "Energy deficiency impairs resistance training gains in lean mass but not strength: A meta-analysis and meta-regression." *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 32(1):125–137. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696/

**Estimating maintenance.** Use the **Mifflin-St Jeor** equation for resting metabolic rate — the most accurate common equation in the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics' systematic review (within 10% of measured RMR for ~82% of non-obese adults) — then multiply by an activity factor (~1.4–1.6 sedentary-job-plus-training, ~1.7+ active job).
- Mifflin-St Jeor: men RMR = 10×kg + 6.25×cm − 5×age + 5; women − 161.
- Frankenfield D et al., 2005. "Comparison of predictive equations for resting metabolic rate in healthy nonobese and obese adults: a systematic review." *J Am Diet Assoc* 105(5):775–789. https://www.jandonline.org/article/S0002-8223(05)00149-5/abstract

**Adjusting from measured weight change.** Any equation is only a first guess (±10%+). Calibrate empirically: weigh daily under consistent conditions (morning, post-toilet, pre-food), use the **weekly average** to smooth water noise, compare 2–3-week trends. If gaining slower than ~0.25%/week, add ~100–200 kcal/day; faster than ~0.5%/week, subtract the same. The ~7,700 kcal/kg heuristic converts a trend error into a calorie adjustment (e.g., 0.1 kg/week off-target ≈ ~110 kcal/day). This trend-based adjustment is the standard method in the bodybuilding-nutrition literature (Iraki et al. 2019, above).

## 3. The "be healthy" layer: pattern, fiber, food vs supplements

**Dietary pattern.** The Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020–2025 define a healthy pattern: vegetables, fruits, whole grains (≥half of grains), fat-free/low-fat dairy or fortified alternatives, varied protein foods (including seafood, legumes, nuts), oils; limits: <10% of calories from added sugars, <10% from saturated fat, <2,300 mg sodium. Building the surplus out of these foods — rather than discretionary calories — covers micronutrients and long-term disease risk.
- USDA/HHS, 2020. *Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2020–2025.* https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/

**Fiber.** Guideline target **14 g per 1,000 kcal** (≈28 g/day for women, 34 g/day for men at typical intakes); ~90% of Americans fall short. A higher-calorie muscle-gain diet should scale fiber up with it (a 2,800–3,000 kcal gain diet implies ~39–42 g/day).
- Dietary Guidelines 2020–2025 (above); fiber intake shortfall: American Society for Nutrition summary. https://nutrition.org/most-americans-are-not-getting-enough-fiber-in-our-diets/

**Food first, supplements second.** The joint AND / Dietitians of Canada / ACSM position states nutrition goals should be met primarily through food; supplements are adjuncts for specific, evidence-supported cases. Protein powder counts as a food-convenience product, useful only for hitting the daily protein number.
- Thomas DT, Erdman KA, Burke LM, 2016. "Position of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada, and the American College of Sports Medicine: Nutrition and Athletic Performance." *J Acad Nutr Diet* 116(3):501–528. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26920240/

**Supplements with real evidence for muscle gain:**
- **Creatine monohydrate, 3–5 g/day** — per the ISSN position stand, "the most effective ergogenic nutritional supplement available to athletes for increasing high-intensity exercise capacity and lean body mass during training"; long-term use up to 30 g/day for 5 years documented as safe in healthy people; dehydration/cramping claims unsupported. No loading phase needed (3–5 g/day saturates in ~3–4 weeks).
  - Kreider RB et al., 2017. "ISSN position stand: safety and efficacy of creatine supplementation in exercise, sport, and medicine." *JISSN* 14:18. https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-017-0173-z
- **Vitamin D — status-dependent only.** Deficiency impairs muscle function (type II fiber atrophy, proximal weakness), and supplementation reliably raises 25(OH)D; but RCT meta-analyses in athletes show no consistent strength/power benefit once replete (at most a possible lower-limb strength effect in subgroups). Verdict: test (or assume risk if little sun exposure) and correct low status for general health; don't expect hypertrophy gains from supra-sufficient dosing.
  - Zhang L et al., 2019. "Effect of vitamin D supplementation on upper and lower limb muscle strength and muscle power in athletes: A meta-analysis." *PLOS ONE* 14(4):e0215826. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0215826
  - Han Q et al., 2023. "Effects of vitamin D supplementation on maximal strength and power in athletes: systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs." *Front Nutr* 10:1163313. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2023.1163313/full

Most other mass-market supplements (BCAAs, testosterone boosters, mass gainers beyond their calories) lack comparable evidence; the AND/DC/ACSM and ISSN documents above list creatine, caffeine (performance), and protein as the short list with strong support.

## 4. Sleep–nutrition–recovery interplay

**Muscle protein synthesis and hypertrophy.**
- Saner et al.: 5 nights of 4 h/night sleep **reduced myofibrillar protein synthesis ~19%** vs normal sleep in healthy young men; notably, adding high-intensity interval exercise during the restriction period preserved MPS — exercise partially buffers the sleep hit.
  - Saner NJ et al., 2020. "The effect of sleep restriction, with or without high-intensity interval exercise, on myofibrillar protein synthesis in healthy young men." *J Physiol* 598(8):1523–1536. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32078168/
- Lamon et al.: one night of total sleep deprivation reduced muscle protein synthesis (~18%), raised cortisol and lowered testosterone — a catabolic hormonal shift after even a single bad night.
  - Lamon S et al., 2021. "The effect of acute sleep deprivation on skeletal muscle protein synthesis and the hormonal environment." *Physiol Rep* 9(1):e14660. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7785053/
- Body-composition partitioning: in a controlled crossover (14 days, calorie deficit), sleeping 5.5 h vs 8.5 h produced the same weight loss but **55% less of it from fat and 60% more from fat-free mass** — chronic short sleep steers body-composition change in exactly the wrong direction for this goal.
  - Nedeltcheva AV et al., 2010. "Insufficient sleep undermines dietary efforts to reduce adiposity." *Ann Intern Med* 153(7):435–441. https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/0003-4819-153-7-201010050-00006

**Appetite regulation.** Two nights of 4 h sleep in healthy men **decreased leptin ~18% and increased ghrelin ~28%**, with increased hunger (+24%) and appetite specifically for calorie-dense, high-carbohydrate foods. Implication: under sleep deprivation, hunger signals are biased upward and toward junk — track intake rather than trusting appetite, and keep the surplus deliberately small.
- Spiegel K et al., 2004. "Sleep curtailment in healthy young men is associated with decreased leptin levels, elevated ghrelin levels, and increased hunger and appetite." *Ann Intern Med* 141(11):846–850. https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/0003-4819-141-11-200412070-00008

**Pain sensitivity.** Experimental sleep deprivation increases pain sensitivity in healthy people: a meta-analysis found sleep deprivation significantly lowers pain thresholds (hyperalgesia), and the Finan review concludes sleep problems predict new-onset and worsening pain more strongly than pain predicts poor sleep (sleep → pain is the dominant direction). Total sleep deprivation also impairs conditioned pain modulation (the body's own pain-inhibition system). Practical read: post-training soreness and "everything hurts" days will be amplified by short sleep; that amplification is a sleep signal, not necessarily an overtraining signal — but it also argues against stacking hard sessions on badly slept nights.
- Schrimpf M et al., 2015. "The effect of sleep deprivation on pain perception in healthy subjects: a meta-analysis." *Sleep Med* 16(11):1313–1320. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1389945715008965
- Finan PH, Goodin BR, Smith MT, 2013. "The association of sleep and pain: an update and a path forward." *J Pain* 14(12):1539–1552. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4046588/
- Staffe AT et al., 2019. "Total sleep deprivation increases pain sensitivity, impairs conditioned pain modulation and facilitates temporal summation of pain." *PLOS ONE* 14(12):e0225849. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0225849

**Reference target.** The AASM/Sleep Research Society consensus: adults should sleep **≥7 h/night** on a regular basis for optimal health; <7 h is associated with impaired immune function, increased pain, and weight gain among other harms.
- Watson NF et al., 2015. "Recommended amount of sleep for a healthy adult: a joint consensus statement of the AASM and SRS." *Sleep* 38(6):843–844. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26039963/

**Synthesis for this plan.** Chronic sleep deprivation degrades every link in the chain this plan depends on: less MPS per gram of protein eaten, worse calorie partitioning (surplus → fat, deficit → muscle loss), dysregulated appetite that fights a controlled surplus, and amplified pain that can be misread as training failure. Nutrition can hedge (protein at 1.8–2.2 g/kg, small tracked surplus, creatine) but cannot substitute for sleep; sleep improvement is the highest-leverage "nutrition" intervention available here.

## Caveats

- Morton 2018 and the surplus trials are group averages; individual protein needs and partitioning vary — the tracking loop in §2 is the correction mechanism.
- The Helms 2023 surplus trial was small (n=17 completers, COVID attrition) and in trained lifters; beginners likely tolerate the upper end of the 0.25–0.5%/week gain range better.
- Sleep-restriction MPS studies are short-term lab protocols; long-term hypertrophy RCTs under chronic partial sleep restriction don't yet exist, so the hypertrophy claim is mechanistic extrapolation plus the Nedeltcheva body-composition data.
- Nothing here is medical advice; anyone with kidney disease (protein/creatine) or on medication affecting sleep or appetite should involve a physician.
